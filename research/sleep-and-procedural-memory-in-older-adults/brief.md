# Brief: sleep and procedural memory in older adults

I read this topic as being about how sleep (overnight sleep, daytime naps, sleep architecture and microstructure) supports the consolidation of procedural memory in older adults, and why that support seems weaker with age. Procedural memory here means non-declarative skill learning. Its usual synonyms and operationalizations are motor sequence learning, motor skill learning, motor memory, finger-tapping or motor sequence tasks (MST), the serial reaction time task (SRTT), motor adaptation, and cognitive or procedural skills such as the Tower of Hanoi. Sleep-side concepts are NREM and slow-wave sleep, sleep spindles, slow oscillation–spindle coupling, REM sleep, napping, sleep deprivation, and sleep quality. Interventions that modulate sleep to boost consolidation are also relevant: targeted memory reactivation (TMR), closed-loop auditory stimulation, tACS/tSOS, and the timing of training relative to sleep. Ageing-side concepts are healthy older adults (roughly 60+), age-comparison designs (young vs older), and related at-risk groups that are usually compared against healthy older controls (MCI, Parkinson's disease, stroke, OSA). These are in scope when the focus is sleep-dependent procedural or motor consolidation. Out of scope: declarative-only memory studies (word pairs, episodic memory) with no procedural component; sleep and cognition in general with no learning or consolidation paradigm; studies of children or young adults only; animal-only work, except as mechanistic background; and general sleep-disorder treatment trials with no memory outcome. The most useful evidence is experimental human studies, such as sleep vs wake or nap vs no-nap designs, within-subject crossovers, and age-group comparisons with polysomnography/EEG, plus neuroimaging correlates (fMRI, white matter, gray matter). Next come RCTs of sleep-modulation interventions in older adults, then systematic reviews and meta-analyses. These matter especially because the literature is contested: some argue the reported "sleep benefits" are artefacts of fatigue or time-of-day effects. Registered trials are useful for spotting work that is planned or unpublished.
